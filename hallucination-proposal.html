Hallucination / Game proposal / Week 1 / Duncan Sanders

ROOM 412

A clicker game set in a hotel room that starts out full of hallucinations. Every tap raises your Certainty, the room gets sharper, and the hallucinations disappear.

01

The game

Night, rain on the window, a wall clock stuck at 11:58, and a single key card on the bed.
Working title: Room 412

Working title: Room 412

Room 412 is a clicker, or incremental, game. You check into a hotel room late at night with a single key card. Tapping the card raises a number called Certainty, the way clicking a button raises the count in Universal Paperclips.

The visual twist is that the room begins with the maximum number of hallucinated features: everything is blurred and doubled, three clocks disagree, there is an extra lamp, a door that should not be there, and a dark shape standing by the bed. Each tap makes the room a little sharper, and hallucinations disappear one by one at set Certainty levels, until the room is crisp, plain and quiet.

The player is trying to become sure of what is real. The more certain they become, the less strange the room is, and the question the game asks is whether that is a good trade. It takes about three minutes, uses one interaction (tap), and is told through image, sound and silence rather than text.

I chose this game because Room 412 is built on the same core loop. Paperclips starts as plain text and numbers, and a single button grows into a system the player no longer fully controls. The creator says he was inspired by philosopher Nick Bostrom’s paperclip maximizer thought experiment and by Kittens Game, which starts simple and spirals into questions about how societies are structured.

Three meaningful moments

1. The first click

The screen is only words and one button. Pressing it raises a counter, so the player learns the rule with no instructions. This is the model for my opening, where the first tap on the key card raises Certainty to 1.

2. Automation takes over

After buying machines that make clips without clicking, the player’s hand matters less than the system. In Room 412 the room starts to check itself, and the number climbs while the room empties faster.

3. The scale breaks

The game moves from a desk to the universe, and the same goal starts to feel absurd. In Room 412, the window reveals that the room you were certain about may only be a reflection.

How tapping the key card works like clicking for a paperclip

Every part of Paperclips’ loop has a matching part in Room 412. The mechanics are the same, but what the player earns, and what it costs, is reversed: in Paperclips each click adds something to the world, and in Room 412 each tap takes a hallucination away.

03

Critical discussion with AI

Verified research

  • Universal Paperclips was created by Frank Lantz and announced as a free web game on 9 October 2017, with Bennett Foddy as co-programmer and Hilary Lantz helping with the math (Wikipedia).
  • Lantz says incremental games make abstract mathematical relationships feel concrete, and the interface is intentionally minimal, starting as only text and numbers (GamesBeat).
  • The game draws on Nick Bostrom’s paperclip maximizer idea and on Kittens Game (Wikipedia, IF50).

Gaps I found

Gap 1: the first games did not fit

I first researched Samorost and Windosill, which are close to the mood, but both depend on Flash and do not run in modern browsers. Paperclips runs in a browser and shares my game’s core loop.

Gap 2: nothing connects it to hallucination

Every source frames Paperclips around AI risk and incremental design. None mention perception or hallucination. That link is my own interpretation: a goal pursued relentlessly changes the world, and in Room 412 the goal is certainty.

Gap 3: sound is unverified

I found no source about the game’s audio, so I make no claims about it and will design my own sound language.

Selected exchange

I could not find any creator statement about hallucination, or about the game’s sound. Connecting it to hallucination would be an interpretation, not a documented intention.

My conclusion and design decision

What I learned: Paperclips shows that one repeated action can carry a whole experience, and that the game gets stranger when the rules stay the same but their meaning shifts. What I decided: Room 412 keeps the clicker loop but reverses the reward. In Paperclips, each click adds something to the world. In Room 412, each tap raises Certainty and removes a hallucination, so the player is rewarded with a clearer, plainer, emptier room and has to decide whether that is what they wanted.

04

Narrative and concept

My reading of hallucination

Hallucination is perception that feels fully real while contradicting everything else. In Room 412 it is not a jump scare. It is a room where the clocks disagree, the lamp is doubled, and the edges will not hold still. Certainty is the only way to make it stop.

The world

A hotel room at night, in the rain. A wall clock, a lamp, a phone, a bed and a window. At the start there are twelve hallucinated features layered on top of them.

The player’s role

A guest who has just checked in and wants to be sure of the room. Their only power is to tap the key card, which raises Certainty and sharpens what they see.

The experience

A calm, compulsive pull toward clarity, like squinting until the world comes into focus. By the end the room is clear and plain, and the player should wonder whether they miss the strangeness.

  1. OpeningThe room is at its most hallucinated. The key card glows. The first tap shows Certainty: 1 and the blur loosens slightly.
  2. Stage 1: Check-inTaps sharpen the picture. Blur and double vision fade, and the first four hallucinations go.
  3. Stage 2: The roomAttention runs low, so the player pauses to listen to the rain. They can spend Certainty to dismiss a chosen hallucination early, and from Certainty 50 the room starts to sharpen itself.
  4. Stage 3: The windowThe room is almost clear. The last hallucinations live in the window and the reflection, and the player taps rain on the glass to clear them.
  5. EndingThe room is sharp, plain and silent. The clock strikes midnight. The player can stay, or tap the key card once more to return to the blurred room.

What it teaches me: rain on glass, blur and light flares can make a night feel dreamlike without any fantasy objects.

Palette

Night navy, hotel-sign red, rain teal, lamp orange and clock gold. These come from my portfolio and my Hotel Clock 3D studio.

Visual

Dark room, warm light. At the start, heavy blur, color-split edges, grain and drifting objects. As Certainty rises, the blur and color split shrink and the grain fades, so the room visibly sharpens. Each hallucinated object dissolves into rain and light rather than vanishing abruptly. Titles use a heavy rounded sans, and a condensed type for the clock and key card.

Interface

No menus. A Certainty number sits in one corner and a thin Attention bar in another. One quiet line says “Check what you trust.” The key card glows when it is ready to tap.

Motion

Slow drifts, rain ripples, focus pulls. Each tap sends a small pulse from the key card, and the picture tightens a little. Motion reduces for visitors who prefer less movement.

Audio

At the start, whispering, rain and a clock tick that slips out of rhythm. As hallucinations disappear, each sound drops away with a soft low tone, until only silence is left in the clear room. Sound starts off, with a visible button to turn it on.

07

Two-week build plan

Week 2: build

Make a playable browser prototype with the clicker loop. Tapping the key card raises Certainty, and a single sharpness value driven by Certainty controls the blur, color split and grain. Add a list of twelve hallucinated elements, each with a threshold at which it dissolves, an Attention bar that depletes and refills, and the ending. Use simple shapes for the room, a rain scene, and a sound toggle with whispers, rain and the clock tick.

Week 3: test and refine

Have at least five people play without instructions. Watch whether they understand tapping and notice the room clearing. Tune the thresholds so the game lasts about three minutes, improve the art and sound, check it on a phone and laptop, and publish the game from this portfolio.
