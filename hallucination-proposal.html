Hallucination / Game proposal / Week 1 / Duncan Sanders

ROOM 412

A clicker game set in a hotel room. Every tap makes you more certain of what is real, and makes something in the room disappear.

01

The game

Night, rain on the window, a wall clock stuck at 11:58, and a single key card on the bed.
Working title: Room 412

Working title: Room 412

Room 412 is a clicker, or incremental, game. You check into a hotel room late at night with a single key card. Tapping the card raises a number called Certainty, the way clicking a button raises the count in Universal Paperclips. The twist is visual: each tap also makes something in the room fade away, first a picture frame, then a book, then the lamp, until the hotel is almost empty.

The player is trying to become sure of what is real. The more certain they become, the less is actually there. The game takes about three minutes, uses one interaction (tap), and is told through image, sound and silence rather than text.

Case study: Universal Paperclips

Game I played

Universal Paperclips, a free browser game released October 2017.

I chose this game because Room 412 is built on the same core loop. Paperclips starts as plain text and numbers, and a single button grows into a system the player no longer fully controls. The creator says he was inspired by philosopher Nick Bostrom’s paperclip maximizer thought experiment and by Kittens Game, which starts simple and spirals into questions about how societies are structured.

Three meaningful moments

1. The first click

The screen is only words and one button. Pressing it raises a counter, so the player learns the rule with no instructions. This is the model for my opening, where the first tap on the key card raises Certainty to 1.

2. Automation takes over

After buying machines that make clips without clicking, the player’s hand matters less than the system. In Room 412 the room starts to check itself, and the number climbs while the room empties faster.

3. The scale breaks

The game moves from a desk to the universe, and the same goal starts to feel absurd. In Room 412, the window reveals that the room you were certain about may only be a reflection.

03

Critical discussion with AI

Verified research

  • Universal Paperclips was created by Frank Lantz and announced as a free web game on 9 October 2017, with Bennett Foddy as co-programmer and Hilary Lantz helping with the math (Wikipedia).
  • Lantz says incremental games make abstract mathematical relationships feel concrete, and the interface is intentionally minimal, starting as only text and numbers (GamesBeat).
  • The game draws on Nick Bostrom’s paperclip maximizer idea and on Kittens Game (Wikipedia, IF50).

Gaps I found

Gap 1: the first games did not fit

I first researched Samorost and Windosill, which are close to the mood, but both depend on Flash and do not run in modern browsers. Paperclips runs in a browser and shares my game’s core loop.

Gap 2: nothing connects it to hallucination

Every source frames Paperclips around AI risk and incremental design. None mention perception or hallucination. That link is my own interpretation: a goal pursued relentlessly warps the world, and in Room 412 the goal is certainty.

Gap 3: sound is unverified

I found no source about the game’s audio, so I make no claims about it and will design my own sound language.

I could not find any creator statement about hallucination, or about the game’s sound. Connecting it to hallucination would be an interpretation, not a documented intention.

My conclusion and design decision

What I learned: Paperclips shows that one repeated action can carry a whole experience, and that the game gets stranger when the rules stay the same but their meaning shifts. What I decided: Room 412 keeps the clicker loop but reverses the reward. In Paperclips, each click adds something. In Room 412, each tap raises a number and removes something, so the player is rewarded for making the world less real.

04

Narrative and concept

My reading of hallucination

Hallucination is not chaos. It is perception that feels fully real while contradicting everything else. In Room 412 nothing is obviously wrong. Things simply stop being there, and the player feels more sure the whole time.

The world

A hotel room at night, in the rain. A wall clock, a lamp, a phone, a bed, a picture frame, and a window whose reflection shows a second version of the room.

The player’s role

A guest who has just checked in and wants to be sure of the room. Their only power is to tap the key card, which raises Certainty and quietly erases something.

The experience

A calm, compulsive pull, like staying awake too late. By the end the player should wonder whether being certain was worth what it cost.

05

Interaction and player journey

One main interaction: tap to raise Certainty. Each tap raises the number, and something in the hotel fades.

  1. OpeningBlack screen, rain. The key card glows. The first tap shows Certainty: 1 and a picture frame fades.
  2. Stage 1: Check-inCertainty 0 to 25. Taps make small things vanish one by one. The clock reads 11:58, but its ticks disagree.
  3. Stage 2: The roomCertainty 25 to 100. Attention runs low, so the player pauses to listen to the rain. They spend Certainty to keep one object solid while the rest fade, and the room begins to check itself.
  4. Stage 3: The windowCertainty 100 to 200. The room is almost empty. The window shows a second, full room, and the player chooses which one to believe.
  5. EndingThe last object to vanish is the key card. The clock strikes midnight, the lights go out, and the opening returns with one detail changed.

What it teaches me: rain on glass, blur and light flares can make a night feel dreamlike without any fantasy objects.

Palette

Night navy, hotel-sign red, rain teal, lamp orange and clock gold. These come from my portfolio and my Hotel Clock 3D studio.

Visual

Dark room, soft grain, warm light. Objects do not disappear abruptly. They blur, lose color, and dissolve into rain and grain, so the loss feels like a memory fading. Titles use a heavy rounded sans, and a condensed type for the clock and key card.

Interface

No menus. A Certainty number sits in one corner and a thin Attention bar in another. One quiet line says “Check what you trust.” The key card glows when it is ready to tap.

Motion

Slow drifts, rain ripples, focus pulls. Each tap sends a small pulse from the key card, and one object blurs out in response. Motion reduces for visitors who prefer less movement.

Audio

Rain ambience and a clock tick that slips out of rhythm as Certainty rises. Each fading object has a soft, low tone. Silence marks the moment the room becomes empty. Sound starts off, with a visible button to turn it on.

07

Two-week build plan

Week 2: build

Make a playable browser prototype with the clicker loop: tap the key card to raise Certainty, a list of objects that fade one by one, an Attention bar that depletes and refills, and thresholds that unlock the room and the window. Add a simple rain scene with placeholder shapes, the ending, and a sound toggle.

Week 3: test and refine

Have at least five people play without instructions. Watch whether they understand tapping and notice the objects disappearing. Tune the numbers so the game lasts about three minutes, improve the art and sound, check it on a phone and laptop, and publish the game from this portfolio.
